# Breathing Through Stress

Settle somewhere safe enough to pause. Sit, lie down, or stand with support. Keep your eyes open if that helps you stay oriented. This practice does not ask you to breathe deeply or make stress disappear. You can change position, return to ordinary breathing, or stop whenever you choose.

[Pause 10 seconds]

Begin with contact rather than breath. Feel the floor under the feet, the chair beneath you, or another surface holding some of your weight. Notice one place where support is already present without effort from you.

[Pause 15 seconds]

Look around and let your eyes find three ordinary shapes or colors. Nothing needs to be beautiful or meaningful. Let the room be part of the practice, so attention does not have to stay entirely inside the body.

[Pause 15 seconds]

Now notice that breathing is happening. Do not improve it. The breath may be quick, shallow, uneven, held, or hard to locate. Begin with the breath you actually have.

[Pause 18 seconds]

Choose one place where breathing is easiest to sense: air at the nostrils, movement in the chest, the sides of the ribs, or the abdomen meeting clothing. If none of these feels clear, feel a hand resting on the body and notice the movement through the hand.

[Pause 20 seconds]

Stay alongside one inhale from beginning to end. Then one exhale. There is no need to count or lengthen either one. Let the next breath arrive in its own time.

[Pause 25 seconds]

Stress can make the breath feel like another task. If you notice yourself trying hard, widen attention. Include the surface beneath you, sounds in the room, and the space around the breath. Breathing can remain in the middle of a larger, steadier field.

[Pause 25 seconds]

On the next few exhales, see whether the jaw, hands, or shoulders release a small amount of unnecessary effort. Do not push them down or open. Keep the tension that feels useful. Let go only where the body offers a little room.

[Pause 25 seconds]

Notice where stress is most obvious right now. Perhaps heat, pressure, movement, tightness, restlessness, or a crowded stream of thought. Name the sensation simply, without deciding what it means. Then return to one point of support.

[Pause 25 seconds]

Move gently between them. One breath with the stressed place. One breath with the feet, seat, back, or hand. You are not avoiding the difficult sensation and you are not staring at it. You are practicing a wider attention that can hold both.

[Pause 30 seconds]

If it feels comfortable, allow the exhale to finish without hurrying the next inhale. Do this once or twice, not as a rule. If any breath change brings strain, dizziness, or discomfort, return immediately to ordinary breathing and the support beneath you.

[Pause 25 seconds]

Silently offer a plain phrase: this is a stressed moment. Or: I can meet one breath at a time. Use words only if they reduce effort. Otherwise, stay with sensation and sound.

[Pause 20 seconds]

Let attention include the whole body. Notice that the body is larger than the most activated place. Feel its outline, its contact with clothing, and the air touching exposed skin. Stress may still be here. It does not have to occupy every part of awareness.

[Pause 25 seconds]

Consider the next few minutes after this practice. Is there one small action that would make them more workable: drinking water, reducing one demand, stepping outside, asking for help, or writing down the next task? You do not need to solve the entire situation.

[Pause 20 seconds]

Return to the natural breath for three quiet cycles. Notice each inhale arriving and each exhale leaving. Let the pauses be whatever they are.

[Pause 20 seconds]

Orient outward again. Look toward light, color, and the edges of the room. Move the fingers and feet. Keep any steadiness that is available, without requiring yourself to feel calm.

When you are ready, continue at a pace that respects the body you have now.
