# Spacious Attention

Settle into a seated or resting position that supports both ease and alertness. This practice explores a center and a wider field. Nothing needs to disappear for attention to become steady.

[Pause 12 seconds]

Begin with the body as a whole. Feel its weight, shape, and contact with support. Let sounds and light be present without deciding which one matters most.

[Pause 25 seconds]

Choose one simple center for attention: breath, one hand, or contact beneath the feet. Let it be clear enough to find again and neutral enough to stay with gently.

[Pause 25 seconds]

Follow the center for three natural breaths. Notice the details that change and the details that seem steady. There is no need to narrow the mind around them.

[Pause 35 seconds]

Now include the nearest surrounding sensation. If breath is central, include the chest or belly. If a hand is central, include the arm and the space around it.

[Pause 30 seconds]

Keep the center recognizable while the field becomes wider. Think of a lamp lighting one object clearly without making the rest of the room dark.

[Pause 35 seconds]

Include sounds. Let the closest and farthest sounds arrive within awareness. The center remains available, and hearing does not have to compete with it.

[Pause 35 seconds]

Notice when attention jumps entirely toward a sound or thought. Return to the center, then gently reopen the wider field. This movement can happen many times.

[Pause 40 seconds]

Include the whole body now: face, shoulders, torso, arms, legs, and contact with support. Keep one small place in clearer focus while the rest is held more softly.

[Pause 40 seconds]

Perhaps effort appears as staring inward, holding the breath, or tensing the forehead. Let the gaze soften. Let breathing continue. Spacious attention is receptive, not rigid.

[Pause 35 seconds]

For a few moments, alternate deliberately. Narrow toward the chosen center. Then widen to body and room. Narrow. Then widen. Learn the felt difference without declaring one better.

[Pause 50 seconds]

Now stop alternating and allow both scales at once. The center can be precise. The field can be broad. Attention does not have to choose only one size.

[Pause 40 seconds]

A thought may arise in the field. Notice its presence without entering the whole story. Let it be one event among breath, pressure, sound, temperature, and light.

[Pause 35 seconds]

If an emotion is present, include only as much as feels manageable. Keep contact with the supporting surface and with sounds outside the body. You may return to a smaller neutral center whenever needed.

[Pause 40 seconds]

Notice the edges of the visual field through closed or open eyes. Without moving toward anything, sense that experience extends beyond the point you are focusing on.

[Pause 35 seconds]

Bring to mind one ordinary task that benefits from this quality: reading, listening, writing, cooking, or speaking with someone. Imagine keeping the task central while still noticing body and surroundings.

[Pause 35 seconds]

Return to immediate sensation. Center and field. Detail and context. Neither has to be held perfectly. Each time the mind collapses into one thing, gently make room again.

[Pause 45 seconds]

Let the center become less emphasized. Rest with the whole field for several breaths: sounds, body, space, and the changing movement of attention itself.

[Pause 45 seconds]

Choose the center once more and feel how easily it can be found after widening. Clear focus has not been lost. It now lives within more context.

[Pause 30 seconds]

Begin to close the practice by feeling both feet or the full area of support. Notice the room clearly. Let your eyes open more fully if they were closed.

[Pause 25 seconds]

Move in a way that feels useful. You practiced keeping one thing important without pretending it was the only thing present. Carry that balance into one next task.
